I put drip irrigation into our raised beds on June 13, about a month after everything was planted. That is the wrong order. Every guide assumes empty beds and straight rows. I had beets, tomatoes, peppers and potatoes already up, so the line went in snaking around stems, and in the round beds it went in as a coil.

It works anyway. Seven beds, one timer zone, water at the roots every morning without me standing there with a hose.

## The beds and the layout

Four 8' x 4' beds and three 3' round beds, all corrugated metal, all on a single zone. That is about 149 square feet of growing space.

The water comes from a hose bib, runs through the head assembly (below), then goes underground in plain blank tubing. I trenched it 3 to 4 inches deep across the wood-chip path from a post to the beds. At each bed a tee splits off a branch that climbs the outside wall and hooks over the rim with an elbow, so there are no holes drilled in the metal.

Inside the beds it is emitter tubing: 17mm line with a built-in emitter every 12 inches. In the rectangular beds it runs 4 rows with a clamp at the end. In the round beds it is a coil.

Each rectangular bed has four 7' runs with about 1.5' of line to make each bend, so roughly 32.5' per bed. Each round bed has a little under 5' coiled. That is about 140' of emitter line across all seven beds, and 140 emitters.

## Sand under the line, mulch on top

Before backfilling the trench I laid down a bed of sand under the line. Our soil is full of rocks and gravel, and a rock rubbing against poly tubing every time the ground heaves is how you get a leak you cannot find. The sand gives the line something soft to sit in. Mulch goes on top of everything in the beds.

The one leak I did get was at a fitting, not the line. The T-connection and 90 degree elbows rely on a pressure fitting a bad seal and it will leak water immediately. Take it back apart and reset to test again.

## Filter and pressure regulator, and why you need both

House water pressure is usually somewhere around 40 to 60 PSI. Drip line is built for much less, and too much pressure blows fittings apart and pushes emitters past their rated flow. The regulator holds the line at 30 PSI no matter what the house is doing.

The filter matters because an emitter's opening is tiny. One grain of sand is enough to plug it, and a plugged emitter in the middle of a bed looks like a dead plant three weeks later, not a clog.

The order at the spigot is **timer, then filter, then backflow regulator (big box store), then pressure regulator**, then the adapter into the line. 

## Soaker hose vs drip irrigation

In the video I call it soaker hose. It is not. Soaker hose weeps along its whole length, and how much it puts out depends on pressure and how far the water has traveled, so the far end of a long run gets less. Emitter tubing puts out a fixed rate at each emitter, 0.6 gallons per hour here, from the first foot to the last. On a system snaking through seven beds, that evenness is the whole point.

## The timer

The B-hyve XD is a hose-end timer with four independent zones, controlled from a phone app. The raised beds are zone 4. Zone 3 runs a sprinkler.

I run a schedule based timer, however there is a caveat with the XD Timer, it can only run one zone at a time. So plan your automations around down times on the spigot. For me, that was post sprinkler watering of the remaining parts of the lot. The app will alert you of upcoming rains and cut off watering if enabled.

## How long to run drip irrigation

The usual rule is about an inch of water a week. That is not what my beds needed.

I spent the season watching the beds instead of trusting a number. By July, if the beds did not get water in the morning, the plants were visibly wilting by early afternoon. What I landed on was **an hour every morning, and on the hottest days a second hour-long cycle**. Anything less did not keep up with the amount of plant we had packed in.

Here is what an hour actually delivers:

- Flow per hour = feet of emitter line x 0.6 gallons. 140' of line is about 84 gallons an hour.
- One inch of water over 149 square feet is about 93 gallons.
- So one hour is about 0.9 inches across the beds, and a two-cycle day is about 1.8.

A week of single morning runs is over 6 inches, before counting a single hot-day double. That is six times the rule, and the plants still told me it was the minimum. Drip puts that water at the roots instead of across the whole surface, so none of it is going to the paths, but it is still a lot of water.

The beds do not all get the same share, either. A rectangular bed gets about 1 inch an hour. A round bed, with less than 5' coiled into 7 square feet, gets closer to 0.6. If I were doing the rounds again I would put more line in them.

Your number will be different. Full-sun metal beds dry out faster than in-ground rows, a packed bed drinks more than a sparse one, and sandy mix drains faster than heavy soil. Start with a shorter run, then watch the plants at 2pm on a hot day. They will tell you.

## The sensors, and what they cannot tell you

Each bed has an Ecowitt WH51 soil moisture sensor, reporting into Home Assistant and into the morning briefing from [the house brain](/archive/hestia-house-brain-build-log/). I used them alongside the weather forecast and a walk past the beds, not instead of either.

A dead sensor looks exactly like a steady one. In June the base station stopped reporting for six days, and Home Assistant kept showing the last value it had, so the dashboard looked calm the whole time. In August one bed's sensor died and sat there unnoticed for a month. If the timer had been taking orders from those readings, it would have kept watering off numbers that were days old.

The readings also do not compare across beds. Two beds on the same zone, same schedule, sat around 34% and 51% all week. That gap is soil mix and where each sensor sits, not one bed being wetter. The number is useful for watching one bed change, not for ranking them.

## What is next

The plan is to close the loop and let the sensors drive zone 4 directly. Before that happens, the system has to notice when a sensor goes quiet. The morning briefing now flags any sensor that stops reporting, reads zero, or holds the same number for a full day, and a frozen reading will have to clear that same check before it gets anywhere near the valve.

Before the ground freezes I will flush the lines, bring the timer, filter and regulator inside, and blow the line out with a compressor under 40 PSI, because the check valves that keep it full all summer keep water in it all winter too. I will document that clean out when I break the beds down.

If you are building one of these in spring, lay the line before you plant. I would still do it a month late over not doing it at all.
