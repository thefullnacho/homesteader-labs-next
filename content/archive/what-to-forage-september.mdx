September is the hinge. The last of the soft fruit is still hanging while the first nuts start to drop, and for about three weeks you can fill a bag with both in the same afternoon. It's also the month the woods stop being generous by accident, black walnut hulls stain for a reason, and one common vine has a lookalike that isn't a stomachache, it's a hospital visit.

Same rule as every month: in season does not mean safe to guess on. Every plant below still gets the full identification, the whole plant, not a berry held up to a phone. The [wild berry guide](/archive/wild-berry-guide/) has the discipline if you need the refresher.

## The nationwide list

### Black walnuts

Green husks are starting to soften and drop, and where they land, the ground goes black within a day, that's the juglone and the tannins doing what they do to hands, driveways, and anything porous. Husk them in gloves outdoors, never on a surface you like. The nut inside is real food, worth every bit of the mess, but the hulling is the whole job. If a squirrel got there first, you'll know, the husks are already open and hollowed.

### Pawpaws

Peak, in the river bottoms and forest edges of the Midwest, Mid-Atlantic, and Southeast. Custardy, tropical-tasting, and they bruise if you look at them wrong, which is why you will never see them in a grocery store. Ripe means it gives like a ripe peach and smells sweet at the stem end, not green and hard. They drop when ready, so the ground under a pawpaw patch is worth checking before the tree.

### Wild grapes

Ripening now on fences, riverbanks, and forest edges across most of the country, from fox grapes in the East to muscadines still finishing in the South. Woody vine, shredding bark, tendrils opposite the leaves, and a cluster of round berries each holding several pear-shaped seeds. This is the one with a dangerous stand-in, see below before you pick a handful for jam.

![A wild grape cluster mid-ripen, dusty purple-black fruit next to berries still small and green on the same stem.](/images/wild-grape-ripening-cluster.jpg)

Notice the same cluster holding both colors at once. September ripens unevenly, so don't write off a vine because half of it still looks like next month's fruit.

### Hen of the woods

The mushroom of the month if you're anywhere near old oaks. Maitake fruits at the base of the same oak trees, often the same tree, year after year, a grey-brown rosette of overlapping caps that can weigh several pounds. It has no dangerous lookalikes at that size and shape, which is rare in mushrooms and part of why it's the one beginners are pointed toward first. Still: the standard caution below applies to everything else growing near it.

### Rose hips

The fruit left behind after the petals drop, swelling on wild and rugosa roses through September. Small, tart, loaded with vitamin C, and safe across the genus, there is no toxic rose hip. The only work is patience with the seeds inside, which are fine to eat in small amounts but usually strained out for tea.

![A rose hip still green, sepals intact, photographed in early September before it's turned.](/images/rose-hip-green-immature.jpg)

This one hasn't ripened yet, still green with the sepals standing up like a little crown. It'll redden through the rest of the month, that patience is the only real work this plant asks of you.

### Acorns

Starting to drop in the Southeast and California oak woodlands, later further north. Every acorn is technically edible after leaching, the tannins are the only obstacle, not toxicity, but leaching is real work: shell, grind or slice, and rinse in water changes until the bitterness is gone. Not a first-timer's project for one afternoon, but worth knowing the timing is now.

## Regional callouts

**Northeast.** Black walnuts and hickories dropping, beach plums finished, wild apples softening on abandoned homestead trees, and hen of the woods flushing hard after any September rain. Rose hips along the coast are at their fattest.

**Southeast.** Pawpaws at their absolute peak, muscadines and scuppernongs the size of golf balls, and persimmons starting in the Deep South, though most varieties still want a frost or a hard squeeze test before they're worth the trouble.

**Midwest and Plains.** Black walnuts everywhere there's a fence line, pawpaw patches in the river bottoms, wild grapes heavy enough to bend the vine, and the first hickory nuts starting to let go.

**West and Pacific Northwest.** Chanterelle season ramping up hard with the first fall rains, huckleberries finishing at higher elevation, and acorns dropping across the California oak savannas, one of the few regions where acorn processing has a living, unbroken tradition worth learning from rather than reinventing.

## The September mixup, and this one is not a stomachache

Canada moonseed climbs the same fences and riverbanks as wild grape, ripens the same dusty blue-purple, and grows in clusters that pass for grapes at a glance. It isn't one. Moonseed berries carry a single flattened, crescent-shaped seed, grape berries carry several small pear-shaped ones. Moonseed has no tendrils, grape always does. And moonseed leaves are usually smooth-edged where the leaf stalk attaches near the center of the blade, while grape leaves are toothed with the stalk meeting at the base like a normal leaf.

Cut one berry open before you trust a whole cluster. One crescent seed, not grape, put it down. This mixup shows up in poison control call logs every fall, and it is entirely preventable with thirty seconds and a fingernail.

![A wild grape berry split open, showing a pale teardrop-shaped seed embedded in the flesh, nothing like moonseed's single flat crescent.](/images/wild-grape-cut-seeds.jpg)

That shape is the whole test. Pointed, teardrop, off to one side of the pulp, that's grape. A single seed lying flat and curved like a sliver of moon is the one to put down.

## A word on September mushrooms

Hen of the woods is the beginner-safe entry point this month, but chanterelles and chicken of the woods are also flushing, and the same rule from every other month applies: mushrooms are a different discipline with different rules, spore prints and gill structure and a local club willing to check your basket. [Mushroom foraging 101](/archive/mushroom-foraging-101/) is the place to start if this is new.

And if you want a second opinion in the field, [our forager](https://build-small-hackathon-forager-field-notes.hf.space/) runs free in your browser and refuses to guess when it isn't sure. That refusal is the feature.

The hinge only lasts a few weeks, then it's all nuts and root cellars until spring. What's dropping where you are? Tell me what's missing for your region, this guide gets revised every year and gets better with what people actually find.
