Search for what to plant in August and you will get the same list every time. Lettuce, spinach, radishes, kale, maybe broccoli if the writer is feeling ambitious. The lists are not wrong. They are just silent about the thing that matters most if you are growing food for security rather than for the table.

What is any of it actually worth?

We have the numbers, so here they are. Every figure below comes from the same crop data that drives our planting tools, ranked by calories per plant.

## The ranking

| Crop | Days to maturity | Yield per plant | Calories per plant | Storage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cabbage | 75 | 3 lbs | **340** | 60 days |
| Kale | 60 | 1.5 lbs | **333** | 7 days |
| Peas | 65 | 0.5 lbs | 184 | 5 days |
| Cauliflower | 70 | 1.5 lbs | 170 | 14 days |
| Broccoli | 70 | 1 lb | 154 | 7 days |
| Swiss chard | 60 | 1.5 lbs | 129 | 7 days |
| Kohlrabi | 55 | 0.5 lbs | 61 | 30 days |
| Spinach | 45 | 0.5 lbs | 52 | 7 days |
| Beets | 58 | 0.25 lbs | 49 | 14 days |
| Carrots | 70 | 0.25 lbs | 46 | 28 days |
| Daikon radish | 60 | 1 lb | 82 | 90 days |
| Turnips | 55 | 0.35 lbs | 44 | 120 days |
| Lettuce | 50 | 0.5 lbs | 34 | 7 days |
| Radishes | 25 | 1 oz | **5** | 14 days |

A cabbage is worth sixty-eight radishes.

That is the whole article in one line, but stay with me, because the second number in that table matters as much as the first.

## The math nobody puts in the list

An adult eats somewhere around 2,000 calories a day. At 340 calories, your best fall crop is about a sixth of one person's daily food. You would need six cabbages to feed one person for one day.

Six cabbages is most of a bed.

Now compare that to what you could have planted in spring. A single potato plant in the same dataset yields 1,048 calories and stores for 120 days. One potato plant beats three cabbages, and it keeps twice as long.

This is not an argument against fall planting. It is an argument for being honest about what fall planting is for.

## Days to maturity is not the number you need

Here is the mistake that ruins more fall gardens than any pest.

You look up cabbage, see 75 days, count 75 days forward from August 1st, land on October 15th, and decide you are fine. You are not fine.

Autumn days are shorter and cooler than the summer days those maturity figures were measured in. Growth slows as the season closes. The working correction is to **add about 14 days to the listed maturity** for anything you sow in August, and then count back from your first frost date rather than forward from today.

Run cabbage through that properly. 75 days plus a 14 day fall factor is 89 days. Sow on August 1st and you need to stay frost-free until the end of October. In zone 4 or 5 that is optimistic. In zone 7 it is comfortable.

So the real first step is not picking crops. It is knowing your own first frost date, then working backwards. Our [planting calendar](/tools/planting-calendar/) does the counting from your ZIP code, including the fall factor, so you can see which of these actually finish where you live.

## What earns its space

Read the table again with the storage column leading, and a different ranking appears.

**Cabbage** is the only crop here that is good at both jobs. Highest calories, and sixty days of storage. If you plant one thing in August and you are thinking about winter, plant cabbage, and plant it in the first week.

**Kohlrabi** looks weak at 61 calories until you notice it matures in 55 days and stores for 30. It is the crop most likely to actually finish in a short season, and it keeps. Underrated.

**Carrots** are only 46 calories a root, but 28 days of storage and genuine cold tolerance mean they can sit in the ground under mulch well past frost. Carrots are not a calorie crop. They are a schedule crop, food you can leave outside and collect in November.

**Kale** is the exception that earns its reputation. 333 calories, 60 days, and it gets sweeter after frost instead of dying. Poor storage off the plant, but the plant itself is the storage.

One thing to plan for while you are sowing rather than after: cabbage, kale and broccoli are all aphid hosts, and an autumn brassica planting finds cabbage aphid reliably. The trap crop has to go in with them, not later, which is why we wrote [a separate note on aphids](/archive/aphid-control-plan/).

## What does not

**Radishes at 5 calories.** Twenty-five days to grow something with the caloric content of a rounding error. Plant them because they are fast and they taste good and they break up soil. Do not plant them believing you are banking food.

**Lettuce at 34.** Same story. A fall lettuce bed is a pleasure, not a hedge.

There is nothing wrong with either. Just know which column you are filling.

## The honest conclusion

A fall garden is not a calorie garden. It is a storage garden and a fresh-greens garden, and those are both worth having.

Your calories were decided in spring, when you did or did not plant potatoes, winter squash, and beans. If you are arriving at August hoping to make up a food gap, the arithmetic does not work, and any list that implies otherwise is selling you comfort.

What August is genuinely good for is extending the season, filling beds that would otherwise sit empty, and putting a few things in the ground that will still be edible in December. Plant cabbage for the calories, kohlrabi and carrots for the storage, kale for the frost tolerance, and radishes because you want radishes.

## Notes on the data

Every figure here comes from [`content/crops/`](https://github.com/thefullnacho/homesteader-labs-next/tree/master/content/crops), the same files the planting calendar reads. Calories per plant are average yield per plant times caloric density, which assumes a plant that actually finishes. A cabbage killed by frost at day 70 is worth zero.

Herbs are excluded, the same way our caloric ROI tool excludes them. Their calories per 100g are culinary trace amounts rather than dietary staples, so letting them into the ranking produces nonsense like sage outranking winter squash.

This piece originally noted that turnip, rutabaga and daikon were missing from our dataset, and called them the traditional high-calorie autumn roots. They are in now, and that description was wrong. Turnips come in at 44 calories a root and daikon at 82, both well down the table. Rutabaga is the best of the three at 210, and it still does not beat cabbage.

Rutabaga is absent from the table above for a different reason: it needs about 90 days, so an August sowing only finishes in the south. Where it does finish it is the best keeper in the bed by a distance, holding for roughly 180 days against cabbage's 60.
