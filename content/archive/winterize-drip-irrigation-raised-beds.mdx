The lows got close enough this week that the drip line is coming out now instead of late November. **[ALEX: one line on the close call, if you want it.]**

This is the teardown for [the system I built in June](/archive/diy-drip-irrigation-raised-beds/): seven raised beds on one zone of a B-hyve XD hose timer, about 140 feet of emitter line. The check valves that kept that line full all summer are the reason it has to come out. They hold water in it all winter too, and water that freezes in a fitting splits it.

2. Flush the lines. Every end cap off, and water run until it came out clear in every bed. It took about five minutes. **[CLIPS: ~/Downloads/drip-teardown-2026-10-09/, End-cap-disconnect (15:11) and Bed-1 to Bed-6-clear (15:14 to 15:16). 3. Pull the timer, the filter and the pressure regulator off the spigot and bring them inside.
4. Blow the line out with the compressor, under 40 PSI. I couldn't get a seal between the compressor and the tubing, so I held it on by hand for a few minutes. The gauge got to about 10 PSI and fell to near zero. It was enough: air came out the far end of every bed. Low pressure is normal with every end open, since the air leaves as fast as it goes in. What matters is that it reaches the bed farthest from the spigot, because air takes the shortest way out. Next year I'll use a hose-thread adapter for the compressor instead of my hand. **[CHECK BEFORE USING THE PHOTO: in Blow-out.HEIC (15:25) both compressor gauges read about 150 PSI. ## What broke

Two things snagged me all season, and both of them are the timer.

**The scroll wheel flips to hours.** The XD sets run time with a scroll wheel. Roll it past 59 minutes and it sometimes stops counting minutes and starts counting hours. On August 18 the app logged Zone 4, the raised beds, at nearly 900 minutes. That is fifteen hours, and the likeliest explanation is that wheel. **[ALEX: did the beds show it, or did you catch it? One line.]** Read the display before you walk away from it.

**Some Zone 4 runs quit a few seconds in.** The app's Zone 4 history has nine runs between September 7 and 23 that lasted 6 to 18 seconds. Most of them come in bursts: two or three short runs, then a long one a few minutes later that held, on September 7, 10 and 23. That's the shape of me starting it, watching it stop, and starting it again until it took. **[ALEX: confirm. Do you remember these stopping on their own, or stopping some yourself, say while fighting the wheel to land on 59 minutes?]**

The log has two blind spots that matter here. It doesn't record how a run was started, and it can't tell a run that quit from one I stopped by hand.

On September 8 my own Bluetooth script started Zone 4 twice: 10 seconds, then 60. The app logged both as 10 seconds, and the script read the zone as idle by 31 seconds into the 60. Five minutes later the same script ran Zone 3 for the full minute. So Zone 4 cut out when started over Bluetooth too, which takes WiFi off the suspect list.

On teardown day I tried to make it happen: from the wheel at the manifold, from the app over Bluetooth, and from the app over WiFi, each left running about a minute. None of them cut out. The log's first run that day is 10 seconds, and that one was me: I went to water the new transplants on Zone 3 and started Zone 4 by mistake. The log can't tell a mistake from a fault either.

Zone 3 is the control. Same timer, same batteries, a sprinkler I moved around the yard and restarted from the app all morning. From August 29 to September 12, Zone 3 logged one short run in about 36 starts. Zone 4 logged seven in 14. Whatever this is, it lives on Zone 4.

The batteries had been in since spring and read 1.25 volts each when they came out, so most of their charge was gone. Both zones share them, so tired batteries alone don't explain why only Zone 4 quits. Tired batteries plus a valve that takes more to open would. **[VERIFY: 1.25 V per cell, not across the pair.]**

The plan from the build was to let the soil sensors drive Zone 4 on their own. A fault that comes and goes is the worst kind for that, because no test can clear it. The sensors can catch it, though: a real run moves the moisture reading, and a 10-second one doesn't.
