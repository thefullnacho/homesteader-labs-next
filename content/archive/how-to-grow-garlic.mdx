Almost everything in the vegetable garden runs on the same clock. You wait for the last frost, you put things in, you harvest before the first frost, you tidy up. Garlic ignores all of that. It goes in the ground in autumn, while you are pulling the tomatoes out, and it sits through the whole winter doing apparently nothing.

Miss that window and you do not get a smaller crop. You get one clove, swollen into a single undivided ball, which growers call a round. Edible, and not what you planted for.

So this guide leads with the date, because the date is the part people get wrong.

## When to plant garlic, by zone

The working rule is **four to six weeks before your ground freezes hard**. You want the cloves to put out roots and no meaningful top growth before winter shuts everything down.

| Zone | Plant | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 3 to 4 | Late September to mid October | Get it in by the end of September if frosts come early |
| 5 | Late September to mid October | Mid October at the latest |
| 6 | Mid October to early November | The most forgiving window of any zone |
| 7 | Mid October to early November | Softneck starts becoming viable here |
| 8 | Late October to late November | Chill is getting marginal, see below |
| 9 | November 15 to December 15 | Pre-chill required, 4 to 6 weeks |
| 10 | December 1 to January 1 | Pre-chill required, 6 to 8 weeks |

These are windows from [extension and grower guidance](https://cceschoharie-otsego.org/gardening/food-gardening/guide-for-growing-garlic), not dates computed from frost normals, and that distinction is deliberate. I will come back to why in a moment, because our own tool got this wrong and the reason it got it wrong is the most useful thing in this article.

If you want your zone's actual frost dates to sanity-check the window, the [planting calendar](/tools/planting-calendar/) resolves them from your ZIP.

## Why autumn, and not spring

Garlic will grow if you plant it in spring. It simply will not divide.

A garlic clove needs a sustained cold period before its growing point will split into a multi-clove head. Six to eight weeks somewhere between 32°F and 50°F is the usual figure. Without it the apical meristem never receives the signal to differentiate, and the plant puts everything into one undivided bulb.

This is vernalization, the same counter that decides whether a biennial ever flowers and whether a fruit tree breaks dormancy properly. I wrote about [the three cold and daylight clocks the hardiness zone map is silent about](/archive/chill-hours-and-the-zone-map/) a few days ago, and garlic is the cleanest example of the first one on any homestead.

The practical consequence: autumn planting is not tradition. You are putting the clove in the ground so it can spend the winter counting.

## The part most guides get wrong, and so did we

Here is where it gets interesting, and where I have to correct our own software.

Our planting calendar models garlic the way it models everything else, as an offset from the last spring frost. Garlic sits at 180 days before it. In zone 6b, where the last frost is March 15, that lands on September 16, which is close enough to right.

In zone 9b the last spring frost is January 10. Subtract 180 days and the model says **plant garlic on July 14**, then cheerfully describes July as "the previous autumn". Zone 10b comes out at July 5.

Nobody plants garlic in a Florida July. The cloves would rot or sprout into heat that ruins them. The model is not broken in some subtle way; it is a temperate assumption run past the point where it means anything, which is the same failure that puts a January 1 frost date on a zone that has never had frost.

**Two lessons, and the second one is yours rather than mine.** Any planting date derived from last spring frost gets less trustworthy the warmer your zone is, because in a mild winter that anchor drifts into the middle of the previous summer. And when a tool gives you a date that contradicts what you can see out of the window, the window wins.

The fix on our end is in hand. The fix on yours is the next section.

## Zones 9 and 10: you have to fake the winter

If your winter does not deliver six to eight weeks in the cold band, your garlic will not divide, and no planting date solves that. You have to supply the cold yourself.

**Pre-chill the bulbs before planting.** Whole bulbs, not separated cloves. Paper or mesh bag, never sealed plastic. Refrigerator at roughly 35°F to 40°F, away from anything humid.

- **Zone 9:** [4 to 6 weeks](https://garlicgods.com/blogs/garlic-growing-by-state/when-to-plant-garlic-in-florida-a-complete-growing-guide), then plant mid November to mid December
- **Zone 10:** 6 to 8 weeks, then plant December through early January

Separate into cloves only at planting time. And keep the bulbs away from apples and other ethylene producers in the fridge, which is a good habit generally and matters more when the bulbs are going back into the ground.

Softneck varieties are the sensible choice in these zones. They tolerate a shorter chill than hardneck does, which is a lower requirement rather than an exemption.

## Hardneck or softneck

The choice is mostly latitude, and it decides more than flavour.

| | Hardneck | Softneck |
|---|---|---|
| Best zones | 3 to 7 | 7 to 10 |
| Cold needed | More | Less |
| Cloves | Fewer, larger, easy to peel | More, smaller, tightly packed |
| Scapes | Yes | Rarely |
| Storage | 4 to 6 months | 8 to 12 months |
| Braiding | No | Yes |

Hardneck gives you better garlic and scapes, and it wants a real winter. Softneck stores far longer and is what supermarket garlic almost always is. If you are in zone 7 either works, which makes it the one zone where you should grow both and decide for yourself.

**Do not plant supermarket garlic.** It is usually softneck grown for storage rather than for your climate, often treated to suppress sprouting, and it is the most common reason a first attempt disappoints. Buy seed garlic from a grower who will tell you the variety name.

## How to plant

Break the bulbs into individual cloves the day you plant, not before. The papery skin on each clove stays on.

**Depth: two to three inches**, pointed end up, flat basal plate down. Deeper in cold zones, shallower in warm ones. Getting this upside down is survivable but costs the plant weeks of effort.

**Spacing: six inches apart, rows about a foot apart.** Our own crop data says six to eight inches, and the wider end is worth it if you have the room, because bulb size responds to spacing more than most crops do.

Pick the biggest cloves from each bulb and eat the small ones. Clove size going in is the single best predictor of bulb size coming out.

**Then mulch, heavily.** Four to six inches of straw or shredded leaves. In cold zones this prevents the freeze-thaw cycling that heaves cloves out of the ground. In all zones it suppresses the spring weeds that would otherwise compete with a crop that cannot shade them out.

## Through the winter, and the spring

You will see green shoots before winter in most zones. That is normal and not a mistake. Some top growth before dormancy is expected and the plant is fine under snow.

In spring, garlic is one of the first things moving. Keep it weeded, because it competes poorly, and give it consistent water until about three weeks before harvest, then stop. Watering right up to harvest gives you bulbs that store badly.

**Hardneck sends up a scape** in late spring, a curling flower stalk. Cut it once it curls. Left alone it takes energy that would otherwise go into the bulb, and the scapes themselves are one of the best things the garden produces all year. They are worth their own article and will get one.

## When to harvest

This is the second date people get wrong, usually by waiting too long.

**Harvest when about a third to a half of the leaves have gone brown, and the rest are still green.** Each green leaf corresponds to a wrapper layer around the bulb, and those wrappers are what let it store. Wait until everything is brown and the wrappers have already broken down in the soil. The bulbs will split, and they will not keep.

Depending on zone that is roughly June or July in the north, about nine months after an October planting, and May or June in the south, closer to six months after a December one. The southern crop is in the ground for less time, not more, because it starts later and finishes before the heat arrives. Do not pull by the stem. Loosen with a fork first, because the necks snap more easily than you expect.

## Curing and storing

Curing is what turns a harvested bulb into one that lasts.

Hang or rack the whole plants, leaves and roots still attached, somewhere **dry, shaded, and moving with air** for three to four weeks. Never in direct sun. Never in a sealed space. When the necks are dry all the way through and the outer wrappers rustle, trim the roots and cut the stems back to an inch, or leave softneck stems long and braid them.

Store at room temperature in something breathable. **Not the refrigerator**, which is precisely the cold band that tells a clove to start growing again, which is the whole point of this article read backwards.

Set aside your largest bulbs for replanting before you start eating. Every year you do this, the crop adapts a little further to your ground.

## The short version

Order seed garlic in August. Plant in autumn, on the schedule for your zone. If you are in zone 9 or warmer, put the bulbs in the fridge first and stop pretending your winter is going to do it for you. Mulch deep, cut the scapes, harvest while a third of the leaves are still green, and cure it somewhere shaded with air moving.

Then keep the biggest bulbs back for next year, and do it again.
