October is when the plants pull everything underground. Leaves stop feeding the top of the plant and start filling the roots for winter, and the first frost turns starch to sugar in whatever fruit is still hanging. So the month splits in two: the last fruit gets sweeter after the cold, and the roots are worth digging for the first time since spring.

The digging is where this month gets dangerous. The most common poisonous plant in the eastern half of the country keeps most of its poison in a root that looks like a parsnip. More on that below.

Same rule as every month: in season does not mean safe to guess on. Identify the whole plant, every time. The [wild berry guide](/archive/wild-berry-guide/) has the method if you need it.

## The nationwide list

### American persimmons

Peak across the Southeast and the lower Midwest, reaching north into southern New York and Connecticut. An unripe persimmon is so astringent it dries your whole mouth out, which is why everyone who tried one early swears you have to wait for frost. You don't. The frost rule is a myth, some trees ripen well before any frost and some stay chalky after one. What matters is ripeness: a ready fruit is soft, wrinkled, nearly translucent, and usually already on the ground. Pick up, don't pick off.

### Autumn olive

Invasive across the East and Midwest, so pick as much as you want, you're doing the woods a favor. Red berries about the size of a currant, covered in tiny silver speckles, on a shrub whose leaves are silvery underneath. The speckles are the ID feature to learn. Tart and a little astringent in September, noticeably sweeter after a frost, and the berries cook down into a very good fruit leather.

### Hickories and the last of the walnuts

Shagbark and shellbark hickories are dropping now. The husk splits into four clean sections and the nut inside is the best-tasting wild nut in the East, and the most annoying to crack. The last black walnuts are still coming down too, and the September rule stands: gloves, outdoors, never on a surface you care about.

### Sunchokes

Also called Jerusalem artichoke, a native sunflower with knobby tubers underneath that taste like a nutty potato. Dig after the first frost, when the tops die back and the tubers are at their sweetest. Most of the carbohydrate is inulin, which your gut can't digest, so the gut bacteria eat it instead. Some people call them fartichokes. Go easy on the first plate.

### Rose hips, after frost

Still hanging from September and better now. A frost softens the hip and rounds off the tartness. Same as last month, there's no toxic rose hip, and the only work is getting the seeds out.

### Hawthorn

Small red apple-like fruit called haws, on thorny trees in hedgerows and old pastures. The flesh is edible and good in jelly. The seeds are not: like apple seeds, they contain compounds that release cyanide when crushed, so spit them or strain them out. The thorns will tell you what you're dealing with before the fruit does.

### Acorns

Dropping everywhere now, not just in the South and California. Last month's note still holds: every acorn is edible once the tannins are leached out, and leaching is days of water changes, not an afternoon.

## Regional callouts

**Northeast.** Autumn olive heavy on every roadside, wild apples dropping in old orchards, wild cranberries ready in the bogs, beechnuts in a mast year (roast them, raw beechnuts in quantity upset your stomach), hickories, and sunchokes after the first frost.

**Southeast.** Persimmons at their best, the last muscadines, pecans starting late in the month, maypops (passionflower fruit) still wrinkling on the vine, and beautyberry, which is edible but bland enough that it's only really worth making into jelly.

**Midwest and Plains.** Persimmons across the southern half, hickories and the tail of the walnut drop, autumn olive, pawpaws finished in the first week, and sunchoke patches worth marking now while the flowers still show you where they are.

**West and Pacific Northwest.** Chanterelles at their peak with the rains, acorns across the oak savannas, pinyon pine nuts ready in the Great Basin and the Southwest, prickly pear fruit still ripening in the desert, and evergreen huckleberry on the coast, which keeps fruiting well into winter.

## The October mixup: pokeweed, and it's the roots this time

This is the payoff of a plant we've been following since July. In the [wild berry guide](/archive/wild-berry-guide/) the pokeweed stem was orange-pink and the berries were green and pointing at the sky. By October the plant is in full color. The stems are hard magenta, the clusters hang heavy, and the berries are glossy purple-black at exactly the height of a kid's hand.

This is when people confuse it with elderberry or grapes. The ripe cluster looks like food. The two checks from the berry guide still settle it in seconds: each pokeweed berry is ribbed into segments like a tiny pumpkin, and each leaf is a single undivided blade with a smooth edge. Elderberry has compound leaves with toothed leaflets, and grapes have tendrils. Birds eat pokeweed berries without harm, which is how the plant ends up in every fence line. That tells you nothing about whether you can.

The berries aren't the worst part. Every part of pokeweed is toxic and the root is the most toxic, a thick white taproot that can grow as big as your forearm. October is when people go digging for burdock, sunchokes, and horseradish, and pokeweed root has been dug up and eaten in mistake for parsnip, horseradish, and even ginseng. It causes severe vomiting and diarrhea, and bad cases end up in the hospital.

The fix is the same one that works on the berries: identify the plant before you dig, while it still has leaves and stems to identify. A root on its own, pulled from a patch you didn't look at first, is the thing never to eat. Pokeweed dies back after a hard frost, but the magenta stalks usually stay standing through the winter. If you see them in a patch you're about to dig, move on.

## The second mixup: chestnuts and buckeyes

Chinese chestnuts planted in old yards and parks are dropping now, and so are horse chestnuts and buckeyes, which are toxic. The husks are the tell. A true chestnut husk is covered in dense, needle-sharp spines, like a sea urchin, and holds two or three nuts, each flat on one side with a pointed tip and a little tassel. A horse chestnut husk has a few scattered stubby spikes, and a buckeye husk is nearly smooth. Both hold a single round, glossy nut with no point. Point and tassel, eat it. Round and smooth, put it down.

## A word on October mushrooms

Oysters come up on dead hardwood with the first cold snaps, and hen of the woods is still flushing at the base of old oaks. So are jack-o'-lanterns: bright orange clusters on wood that get picked as chanterelles every year and make people very sick. Mushrooms are their own discipline, and [Mushroom foraging 101](/archive/mushroom-foraging-101/) is the place to start.

If you want a second opinion in the field, [our forager](https://build-small-hackathon-forager-field-notes.hf.space/) runs free in your browser and refuses to guess when it isn't sure.
