Some pests arrive on a schedule. Squash vine borers emerge within a predictable window, and if you know your accumulated heat you can very nearly circle a date on a calendar.

Aphids are not like that. They do not emerge, they accumulate. There is no single arrival to warn you about, which is exactly why so much aphid advice is useless: it is written for someone who already has them, standing over a curled leaf, looking for something to spray.

By then your options are worse than they were in August.

## Why the usual advice underperforms

**Spraying wins the week and loses the month.** Insecticidal soap and horticultural oil work on contact, and they work on whatever else they contact. Ladybird larvae, lacewing larvae, hoverfly larvae, parasitic wasps, all of them are softer targets than the aphids you were aiming at.

Then the rebound. Aphids reproduce without mating and give birth to live young, so a single survivor is a colony again in a fortnight. Your predators cannot restart that fast. You have cleared the leaf and removed the thing that was keeping it clear, and week three is worse than week one.

There is a place for a targeted spray on a badly hit plant. There is no place for it as a strategy.

**Bought ladybirds mostly leave.** You can order them by the pint, release them at dusk, water the bed first, do everything the packet suggests, and most of them will be gone within a day or two. Released adults disperse. It feels like action and it is mostly a donation to your neighbours.

The predators worth having are the ones that arrived on their own and decided to stay. Which brings us to the two things that actually move the needle.

## Give them something better to eat

Aphids have preferences, and they are strong enough to exploit. Plant something they like more than your crop, at the edge of the bed, and a large share of them will settle there instead.

Nasturtium is the one that shows up consistently. In our own companion data it is the recommended aphid trap for every single host crop we track:

## The half of it nobody tells you

**A trap crop you leave in place stops being a trap.**

This is the failure mode, and it is common. You plant nasturtium, it does its job, it loads up with aphids, and you leave it there because it is flowering and it looks good and pulling it feels like undoing your own work. What you now have is a breeding reservoir at the edge of your brassicas, producing generation after generation, each one a short flight from the crop you were protecting.

The instruction in every one of those data entries is the same: remove and discard when loaded. Not compost, since you will just move the colony. Bag it, or drown it, or burn it.

That is the whole discipline. Plant the sacrifice, then actually sacrifice it. If you are not willing to pull a healthy-looking plant on purpose, trap cropping is not a technique you can use, and you are better off skipping it than doing half of it.

## Cut the supply line

Here is the one that surprises people.

Ants farm aphids. They collect the honeydew aphids excrete, and in exchange they defend the colony, carrying off ladybird eggs and harassing larvae and moving aphids to fresh growth. An aphid colony with an ant escort is a protected colony, which is why some infestations sit there untouched while your garden is visibly full of predators.

Break the escort and the predators you already have get to work. A sticky band around a stem or trunk, or simply following the trail back and dealing with the nest, will do more on a tended colony than anything you spray on it.

This is the highest-leverage thing in this article and it costs almost nothing. It is also the thing least likely to appear in a list of aphid tips.

## Why this is an August problem

If you followed the [August planting note](/archive/what-to-plant-in-august/), you are about to put brassicas in the ground. Cabbage, kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi. Five of the twelve crops in that list.

Cabbage, kale and broccoli are all flagged as aphid hosts in our crop data. Cabbage aphid is a specialist, it overwinters on brassica debris, and it finds a young autumn planting reliably.

So the nasturtium goes in when the brassicas go in, not in September when you find the first curled leaf. A trap crop that is not established yet traps nothing. That is the entire reason this is a planning article rather than a treatment article.

## The plan

1. **Now, with the brassicas.** Nasturtium at the bed borders. It is fast from seed and does not need good soil, which is convenient because you want it slightly stressed and attractive rather than lush.
2. **Weekly, thirty seconds.** Turn over a few leaves on the undersides, where they live. You are looking for the start, not the outbreak.
3. **When the trap loads up, pull it.** Bag it. Replant if there is season left.
4. **Follow any ant trail you see** and deal with the nest. Do this before you consider spraying anything.
5. **Water knocks them down.** A firm jet on the undersides physically removes a large share, and aphids that fall off mostly do not make it back. Free, immediate, harmless to predators.
6. **Spray last, spot only, evening.** If one plant is genuinely overwhelmed, treat that plant. Not the bed.

## What this is not

Trap cropping has better evidence behind it in field agriculture than in home gardens, where plot sizes are small and the borders are close enough that "away from the crop" is a matter of feet. The strong rating in our data reflects consistent recommendation across sources plus supporting study, not certainty about your particular bed.

Expect it to reduce pressure, not eliminate aphids. Nothing eliminates aphids. The goal is a garden where the predator population is intact and the ants are not running security, so that the aphids that do arrive get eaten rather than farmed.

That garden is built in August. Not in September, over a curled leaf, holding a bottle.
