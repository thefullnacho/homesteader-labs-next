I found out about making my own syrup from a YouTube video, deep into last winter, weeks after the window had closed. By the time I understood what I was looking at, there were no leaves on anything. I stood in my own woods unable to answer a simple question: which of these is a sugar maple.

So this year I am doing it properly, and I am starting in August, which feels absurd until you think about it for ten seconds.

Tapping is a February job. Identification is an August job. The tree tells you what it is with its leaves, and in February it has none. Every grey trunk in a winter woodlot looks like every other grey trunk. You can learn bark, and eventually you should, but bark is the hard way and you do not need the hard way when the easy way is standing right there covered in leaves.

This is the guide I am using on my own property this month. The February post will be the actual tapping, including whatever I get wrong.

## The first cut, and it eliminates most of the woods

**Maples branch opposite.** Two twigs, two buds, two leaves, directly across from each other at the same point on the stem. Almost every other tree in North American woods branches alternate, staggering one side then the other.

The families that branch opposite are few enough to memorize as **MAD Cap Horse**: Maple, Ash, Dogwood, Caprifoliaceae, Horse chestnut. That is nearly the whole list.

Stand under a tree, look at a young branch, and check. If the twigs stagger, walk away, it is not a maple and nothing below applies. This single test removes oak, hickory, birch, beech, cherry, sycamore and sweetgum in about two seconds, and sweetgum is the one that fools most beginners because its leaf is star-shaped and vaguely maple-ish. Sweetgum branches alternate and carries spiky seed balls. Not a maple, not tappable, gone.

Same principle as the [wild berry guide](/archive/wild-berry-guide/): you identify the whole plant, not the one feature you happened to notice.

## The sugar maple itself

*Acer saccharum*. What you are looking for:

**The leaf.** Five lobes, roughly as wide as it is long, three to six inches. The important part is the edges. The notches between the lobes, the sinuses, are **U-shaped and rounded**, and the edges between the lobe points are **smooth**. A few big points, and clean margins between them. No fine teeth, no serration, no raggedness.

**The bark.** Young trees are smooth and grey. Mature ones go grey-brown and break into irregular vertical plates that lift and curl outward along one vertical edge, so the trunk looks like it is slowly shedding long flakes. Once you have seen it you will use it in winter.

**The seeds.** Paired samaras, the helicopters, in a tight horseshoe, roughly 60 to 90 degrees apart. Here is the seasonal tell most guides bury: **sugar maple samaras mature and drop in autumn.** Red and silver maple drop theirs in late spring. If you watched helicopters rain down in May, that tree is not a sugar maple.

**In October it will announce itself.** Sugar maples turn hard orange, scarlet and yellow, often all on one tree. If you are reading this late and the leaves are already turning, that is not a problem, that is the easiest week of the year to do this. Go out and look for the orange.

## The one that will fool you, and the test that settles it

**Norway maple** (*Acer platanoides*) is the problem. It is planted along streets and in yards across the whole sugar maple range, it has escaped into the woods, and its leaf looks close enough to a sugar maple that people tap it by mistake every single year.

Break a leaf off at the stem and look at the broken end.

**Norway maple bleeds milky white sap. Sugar maple runs clear.**

That is the whole test. It takes one second, it works all growing season, it needs no experience, and it is unambiguous. Nothing else in this post is as useful.

If you want the supporting evidence: Norway maple leaves are broader than they are long, the lobes carry more and sharper points, the samaras spread nearly flat at almost 180 degrees, and the bark is a tight regular criss-cross that never curls into plates.

You can technically tap a Norway maple. People who have done it generally report thin sap and an off flavour, and it is an invasive tree nobody will thank you for keeping. Learn the milky sap test, and use it on every candidate before you tag it.

## The other maples, which are not mistakes

Three more maples are worth knowing, because they are all genuinely tappable and the only cost is yield.

**Red maple** (*Acer rubrum*). Usually three lobes, sometimes five, and the margins are clearly **toothed** rather than smooth. Sinuses are shallow and V-shaped rather than deep and rounded. Red leaf stems, red buds, whitish leaf undersides. Mature bark goes shaggy and flaky in loose grey plates.

**Silver maple** (*Acer saccharinum*). Five lobes cut so deeply the leaf looks shredded, with a bright silver-white underside that flips visible in wind. Droopier, faster growing, common on wet ground.

**Boxelder** (*Acer negundo*). A maple that does not look like one, because its leaf is compound: three to five separate leaflets on one stem. Young boxelder with three leaflets gets mistaken for poison ivy constantly. Green twigs, often with a whitish bloom. Still branches opposite, which is how you know it is a maple.

All three run sweet. They just run less sweet, and that is arithmetic rather than a verdict:

| Tree | Sap sugar | Sap per gallon of syrup |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar maple | about 2% | roughly 43 to 1 |
| Black maple | about 2% | roughly 43 to 1 |
| Red maple | about 1.5% | roughly 57 to 1 |
| Silver maple | about 1.2% | roughly 72 to 1 |
| Boxelder | about 1.3% | roughly 66 to 1 |

The rule underneath that table is the **rule of 86**: gallons of sap per gallon of syrup equals 86 divided by the sugar percentage. Useful the first time you measure your own sap and find it is not 2%, because most trees are not.

Red maple carries one real catch. It buds earlier than sugar maple, and once a maple buds the sap goes off, turning bitter and vegetal. A red maple season is shorter at the back end. Tap them, just tap them first.

## Measure the trunk while you are there

This is why you carry a tape measure in August rather than just walking around pointing at trees.

Tap counts are set by trunk diameter at breast height, meaning 4.5 feet up. Most people do not own calipers, so measure the **circumference** and divide by 3.14.

| Circumference | Diameter | Taps |
|---|---|---|
| under 31 inches | under 10 inches | none, leave it |
| 31 to 56 inches | 10 to 17 inches | 1 |
| 57 to 78 inches | 18 to 24 inches | 2 |
| over 78 inches | 25 inches and up | 2 to 3 |

The conservative producers' standard allows three taps on the largest trees. Plenty of modern operations cap it at two on everything, on the grounds that the tree is a long-term asset and a marginal third tap is not worth the wound. For a first year on your own land, one tap per tree is a completely reasonable place to start.

A tree under ten inches gets nothing. It has not got the reserves, and you will do it real damage for a trickle.

## Tag it, or you will lose it

February you is going to be cold, in a hurry, and looking at a woodlot with no leaves in it and possibly snow on the ground. February you will not remember which tree it was. I promise this because I have already been February me once.

What works:

- **Flagging tape at eye level**, tied loose so it does not girdle anything. Cheap, visible from distance, degrades in a season or two, which is fine because you will retag.
- **Aluminum tree tags** with a numbered stamp if you want it permanent. Nail them at a slight downward angle with the nail left proud by half an inch, so the tree can grow without swallowing the tag.
- **A GPS pin per tree** on your phone, named with the number on the tag. This is the one that actually saves you, because tape goes missing and snow hides everything under three feet.

Write down the circumference with the pin. Come January you will want to plan tap counts from a list, indoors, with a coffee, rather than by wading around in the snow with a tape measure.

While you are at it, note what the tree is standing in. Open-grown trees with big wide crowns and full sun produce sweeter sap than crowded trees in a closed canopy. Sun on leaves is where the sugar comes from. A fat solitary maple at a field edge is worth more than a taller one packed into the middle of the woods.

## What I do not know yet

This is year one for me. I can identify these trees and I can tell you what the research says about tap counts and sugar content, and that is honestly where my authority stops.

I have not run an evaporator. I do not know what my sap actually tests at, what my ratio really turns out to be, or how badly I will misjudge the weather window. All of that is February's problem and February's post, and I would rather tell you now that I am learning this in public than pretend otherwise and get caught by someone who has been doing it for thirty years.

What I am confident about is the part that has a deadline. The identification is not hard, it is just time-limited, and the window closes when the leaves come down.

## Do it this month

Walk your property. Check for opposite branching. Break a leaf stem and look for milky sap. Measure the trunk at chest height. Tie a ribbon, drop a pin, write the number down.

That is an afternoon of work, and it is the difference between tapping trees in February and standing in the cold the way I did last year, watching a video about something I could no longer identify.
