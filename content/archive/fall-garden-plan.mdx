Fall garden advice is mostly a list of things that tolerate frost. Kale, spinach, carrots, brassicas, a nod to garlic. It is not wrong, and it tells you almost nothing about which of them is worth the bed.

So we ranked them. Twice. And the two rankings almost completely disagree.

## Ranking one: calories per plant

| Crop | Days | Calories per plant | Stores |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cabbage | 75 | **340** | 60 days |
| Kale | 60 | 333 | 7 days |
| Rutabaga | 90 | 210 | **180 days** |
| Peas | 65 | 184 | 5 days |
| Cauliflower | 70 | 170 | 14 days |
| Broccoli | 70 | 154 | 7 days |
| Swiss chard | 60 | 129 | 7 days |
| Daikon radish | 60 | 82 | 90 days |
| Kohlrabi | 55 | 61 | 30 days |
| Spinach | 45 | 52 | 7 days |
| Beets | 58 | 49 | 14 days |
| Carrots | 70 | 46 | 28 days |
| Turnips | 55 | 44 | 120 days |
| Lettuce | 50 | 34 | 7 days |
| Radishes | 25 | 5 | 14 days |

Read that and you plant cabbage. It is nearly seventy times the radish and it keeps for two months.

Carrots go from twelfth to first. Cabbage falls from first to eighth. Kale drops nine places, and broccoli and cauliflower, which looked respectable, collapse to the bottom two.

Nothing changed except the denominator.

## Why they disagree

A cabbage is a big plant that produces one big head on roughly two and a half square feet. A carrot is a tiny plant producing a tiny root, but you can grow forty of them in the same space. The per-plant number rewards individual size. The per-square-foot number rewards density, and density wins by a lot.

Radishes are the clearest case. Five calories a root, which is a rounding error, but planted at two inches they still out-produce cabbage per square foot. The crop we called worthless in the [August planting note](/archive/what-to-plant-in-august/) is worthless per plant and perfectly reasonable per bed.

## So which number should you use

**If you are short of space, use calories per square foot.** Most people are short of space. If you have four raised beds and you want the most food out of them, plant carrots and beets densely and treat the brassicas as a luxury.

**If you are short of time and attention, use calories per plant.** Forty carrots is forty thinnings, a longer harvest, and more washing. Six cabbages is six cuts. When labour is the constraint rather than ground, big plants win, and that is a real constraint in autumn when the daylight is going.

**If you are planning for winter storage, ignore both and look at the last column.**

## The column that actually decides a fall garden

Spring gardens are about production. Fall gardens are about what survives until you need it, and on that measure the ranking changes a third time.

```
Rutabaga    180 days
Turnips     120 days
Daikon       90 days
Cabbage      60 days
Kohlrabi     30 days
Carrots      28 days
everything else, two weeks or less
```

Rutabaga keeps three times longer than cabbage and six times longer than carrots. Turnips are close behind. Neither is impressive on calories, and both will still be edible in February when the kale is a memory.

This is the actual argument for a fall garden. Not that it feeds you in October, when you have plenty anyway, but that it feeds you in January.

**The catch on rutabaga:** it needs about 90 days. Sown in August that only finishes in the south. North of roughly zone 6 it is a July sowing, which means this year's window has probably closed and next year's goes in your calendar now.

## Sowing dates work backwards

The number on the seed packet is not your deadline. Autumn days are shorter and cooler than the summer days those maturity figures were measured in, so growth slows as the season closes.

Add roughly **14 days** to the listed maturity for anything sown after midsummer, then count back from your first frost date rather than forward from today.

Turnips at 55 days become 69. Daikon at 60 becomes 74. Rutabaga at 90 becomes 104, which is why it is not an August crop in the north.

## A plan that uses all three rankings

1. **Densest calories, if space is the constraint.** Carrots and beets, sown thickly, thinned properly. They are the only crops here that clear 500 calories a square foot.
2. **Longest storage, if winter is the point.** Rutabaga where the season allows it, turnips everywhere else. Both hold for months in a cold cellar.
3. **Least work per calorie, if time is short.** Cabbage. One plant, one cut, sixty days of storage.
4. **Frost insurance.** Kale, which sweetens after frost rather than dying, and keeps standing in the bed as its own storage.
5. **Because you want them.** Radishes and lettuce. Neither is banking anything. Both are good.

## Notes on the data

Figures come from [`content/crops/`](https://github.com/thefullnacho/homesteader-labs-next/tree/master/content/crops), the same files the planting calendar reads. Nutrition is USDA FoodData Central, raw, per 100g. Calories per plant are average yield times caloric density and assume a plant that finishes; one killed by frost at day 70 is worth zero.

Calories per square foot use each crop's in-row spacing times its row spacing, or spacing squared for crops grown at equal spacing in beds. Mid-range where a range is given. Real spacing varies with soil and variety, so treat the ordering as solid and the absolute numbers as approximate.

Herbs are excluded, the same way our caloric ROI tool excludes them, because their calories per 100g are culinary trace amounts rather than dietary staples.
