When we moved in three years ago, you could not put a shovel in the ground anywhere on this property without turning up grubs. Not occasionally. Every hole, every time, in numbers that made you stop and look at the shovel.

That is a soil telling you what it is. Compacted lawn, thin organic matter, a shallow ecosystem running on almost nothing. Those grubs also grow up into the beetles that go on to eat the garden, which is its own [separate problem](/archive/japanese-beetle-control/) and a good reason to care.

This spring a mole moved in. I am not trapping it. It is the best news the soil has given me in three years, and this is why.

## What we actually did

Cardboard and woodchips. That is the whole intervention.

We started with patches of lawn we wanted gone. Flatten the cardboard, overlap the seams so nothing finds a gap, wet it down, then bury it under several inches of arborist woodchips. No digging, no tilling, no herbicide. The cardboard smothers the grass and rots away inside a season. The chips sit on top and break down slowly from the bottom.

Then we decided to stop doing it in patches and did the entire back yard, which is where the annual garden, the perennial shrubs, the herbs and the ornamental flowers all live. Everything got covered.

The chips came from [ChipDrop](https://chipdrop.com/), which connects arborists who need to dump a truckload with people who want one. It is usually free. The trade is that you do not choose when it arrives, you do not choose the species mix, and you may get a pile considerably larger than you pictured. Have somewhere for it to go.

The raised beds were built on hügelkultur principles, so there is buried wood underneath them breaking down on its own schedule.

Then we waited, which is the part nobody photographs.

## Three years later

There are earthworms everywhere. Not a few under a rock. You cannot move mulch aside without finding them.

The beds have dropped about three inches, and there are two separate things happening under there.

The first is the buried hügelkultur wood breaking down, which is exactly what hügel beds do. I know it is happening because I can feel it. Pulling potatoes this year, the soil against my hands was warm. Not sun-warm at the surface, warm from underneath, which is decomposition running hot rather than rotting slowly and cold. There is a compost pile buried in my vegetable bed and it is still going three years in.

The second is the worms, and that shows up as texture rather than temperature. The surface changed from soil-with-stuff-in-it into something crumbly and dark that holds together when you squeeze it and falls apart when you poke it. That is castings. There are enough worms out there to watch it happen year over year.

![Close-up of the bed surface: dense dark extruded pellets packed between weathered woodchip fragments. Each pellet is an individual casting, and this is what the top inch of the bed now looks like.](/images/earthworm-castings-close-up.jpg)

So the beds are sinking from below and being rebuilt from above at the same time. Three inches of finished soil that we did not buy, did not mix, and did not turn.

## The test I did not know existed

Here is the part that genuinely surprised me. Earthworm density is a recognized soil health measurement. Not a folk rule, an actual protocol used in agricultural soil assessment.

The version you can run this weekend: dig a cube of soil roughly 20cm on each side, about eight inches, tip it onto a tarp, and break it apart by hand counting worms as you go. Multiply by 25 and you have worms per square meter.

Rough interpretation:

| Worms per m² | Reading |
|---|---|
| Over 45 | Good |
| 15 to 45 | Middling |
| Under 15 | Poor |

In imperial terms, ten or more worms in a square foot of topsoil is an excellent result and under five is a soil with a problem.

Two things matter for doing it honestly. Take more than one sample, because a single lucky hole tells you nothing, and count in spring or after autumn rain when the soil is damp and worms are near the surface. Counting in a dry August will tell you your soil is dead when it is only hiding.

I have not run a formal count on this yard yet, which is an omission I intend to fix this fall now that I know it is a real measurement rather than a vibe.

![Carrot seedlings coming up through woodchip mulch. Dark extruded earthworm castings cover the soil between the chips, alongside scattered white irregular chunks of perlite left over from the original bed mix.](/images/earthworm-castings-carrot-bed.jpg)

A note on that last photo, because someone will ask. The dark extruded pellets are the castings. The white irregular chunks are perlite, left over from what the beds were originally filled with, and it is still surfacing three years later. Worth knowing the difference if you are trying to read your own soil: castings are dark, aggregated and shaped, perlite is white, porous and angular. They are easy to confuse in a photo and impossible to confuse in your hand.

## Why the mole is the point

Most gardening advice treats a mole as a pest to be trapped, and I understand why. They tunnel, they heave seedlings, and the ridges across a lawn look like damage because they are.

But a mole is a predator, and predators do not move into places that cannot feed them. A mole eats close to its own body weight in a day, and it does that year round. It cannot live on a hopeful patch of invertebrates. It needs a dense, sustained, reliable prey base underneath it, every month, or it moves on or dies.

Which raises the obvious objection: we had grubs three years ago, moles eat grubs, so where was the mole then?

I think the answer is that grubs are seasonal and patchy. They pulse with the beetle life cycle and they are concentrated in some places and absent in others. Earthworms are the opposite. They are dense, distributed, and available all year. A grub field is a boom and bust. An earthworm population is an income.

The mole did not show up because we have more bugs. It showed up because the ground finally holds enough steady biomass to support something at the top of that particular food chain, all the way through the year. Nothing else we did produced a signal that clear.

## One mole is an anecdote

A single sighting proves nothing by itself. Soil work runs on a timescale where memory is unreliable, and I have already caught myself misremembering which year we mulched what.

So the mole is also the reason I started keeping dated wildlife sightings in [the house system](/archive/hestia-house-brain-build-log/) instead of in my head. Not because one mole matters, but because ten years of dated sightings is a different kind of object entirely. What shows up, when it showed up, and whether it keeps showing up. That is a record of whether this place is getting richer or poorer, and it is not a thing you can reconstruct later from memory.

Individual observations are anecdotes. The long tail of them is an ecological story, and you only get it if you were writing things down before you knew what they meant.

## What this actually cost

Cardboard, which is free and which you are already throwing away. Woodchips, which were free and delivered. Some hours flattening boxes, and the willingness to have a yard that looked like a construction site for a season.

The expensive input was three years of not seeing much happen. There is a stretch in the middle, somewhere in year two, where the grass is gone and the chips have gone grey and nothing visible is improving, and it is very easy to conclude it did not work and go buy soil. It is working. It is happening under the mulch, on a timescale that does not care about your attention span.

If you want the shortest possible version: cover the ground, feed it something carbon-rich, stop digging, and wait long enough for something to come and eat what shows up.

You will know it worked when something moves in that you were told to trap.
