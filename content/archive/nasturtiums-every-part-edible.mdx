Someone on TikTok asked me how many ways you can eat nasturtiums. Then someone else asked, "But how though? Like a salad?"

Yes, like a salad. And like pesto. And like capers. **Every part of the nasturtium is edible**: the leaves, the flowers and the seed pods. (Just never eat ones that were sprayed.)

Sunday's frost was coming and these guys were on their way out, so we took the whole patch and headed to the kitchen.

## What each part tastes like

- **Leaves:** they look like little lily pads and taste peppery, like watercress. Spicy like a radish, so think of that flavor when you cook with them.
- **Flowers:** garnish, stuffing, or just pull them apart petal by petal into a salad. Nasturtiums are trap flowers (they pull bugs away from your other plants), so rinse them well and check for little bugs.
- **Seed pods:** the green, firm ones become "poor man's capers."

## 1. Nasturtium pesto

I think I actually like it better than regular pesto. We ate some for dinner over chicken tortellini, and froze the rest in little cups as "flavor bombs" for the whole winter.

- 2 packed cups young nasturtium leaves (plus a handful of flowers)
- 1/2 cup nuts or seeds (walnuts, almonds or sunflower seeds), toasted
- 1–2 garlic cloves
- 1/2 cup grated parmesan
- Juice of 1/2 lemon, pinch of salt
- About 1/2 cup olive oil

Blend everything but the oil, then stream the oil in until it's smooth. Taste and adjust the salt and lemon. Fridge for a week with a layer of oil on top, or freeze in small cups or ice cube trays.

**Dinner:** sauté chicken in olive oil and garlic with salt and pepper, then toss it with hot tortellini and a few big spoonfuls of the pesto.

## 2. Poor man's capers (pickled seed pods)

1. Pick the green, firm seed pods (skip the yellow or dry ones). Rinse and pop them off the stems into single pods.
2. Brine: warm 1/2 cup water first (sea salt won't dissolve in cold water), then stir in 2 tablespoons fine sea salt until it dissolves. Soak the pods in it in the fridge for 1 to 3 days. Give the jar a shake.
3. Drain and pack the pods into a small clean jar.
4. Heat 1/2 cup white or cider vinegar with 1 tsp sugar (plus a bay leaf and a few peppercorns if you like) to a simmer, and pour it over to cover.
5. Lid it and keep it in the fridge. Ready in about 2 weeks, and it keeps for months refrigerated. It's a fridge pickle, so no canning needed.

Ours are in the brine now. I'll report back on how they turn out.

## 3. Stuffed nasturtium flowers

**The full version (12 flowers):**
1. Pick 12 perfect flowers the same day you serve them. Dunk them in cool water for a few minutes (any aphids float out) and pat them dry.
2. Pinch out the stamens from the middle.
3. Mix 1/2 cup soft goat cheese (room temperature), 1 tbsp finely chopped herbs (chives, basil, thyme), a pinch of salt and pepper.
4. Spoon or pipe 1–2 tsp into each flower (a zip bag with the corner snipped works).
5. Pull the petals up around the cheese and press lightly. Serve within the hour, or keep them in the fridge.

**My quick hack:** no time to mess with the cheese? Use plain Boursin straight from the box. That's what we did, and it was really awesome.

They don't look fabulous the first time, because that obviously takes some practice. Eat one with a leaf: it's like lettuce and a radish had a baby.

**Even when you screw it up and it doesn't look as pretty as it's supposed to, stuff's delicious.** Cheers! 🌼

## Next year

In Connecticut, nasturtiums are annuals, but they drop seeds that pop back up every spring, and I save the big seeds too. In frost-free places they're perennial.
